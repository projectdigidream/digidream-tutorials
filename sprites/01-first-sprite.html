Your first sprite

ThreeSprite draws characters that live in a 3D world but are pictures: one picture per view, the angle the camera sees them from. You draw each view by hand, a 3D rig moves them all at once, and the studio bakes the result into the sheet a monster or a prop is drawn with.

Views: the one idea to get right

A sprite has a number of views, from 1 to 64. The presets offered are 1, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24 and 32. Each view is the picture a camera sees when it stands at one angle around the character. With 8 views there is one every 45°.

  • View 1 faces the viewer. It is the character seen from the front.
  • The views go anticlockwise seen from above. View k is what a camera sees from (k−1)/N of a turn around the character.
  • So with 8 views, view 3 is the camera at the character's left, and the character appears facing screen-left. View 5 is its back, and view 7 its right side.

This is the same convention Doom uses for its rotations, so Doom-style exports need no remapping.

The game picks a view from where the camera stands relative to where the character heads. The demo below does the same sum as ThreeSprite. Drag the camera round the circle, or turn the character with Facing.

Each view owns a wedge of the circle centred on its own angle. With 8 views, view 1 covers 22.5° either side of straight ahead, and the view swaps halfway between two angles.

Mirror symmetry

Most characters look the same from the left as from the right, flipped. With mirror symmetry on, you draw only ⌊N/2⌋+1 of the N views, and the rest are made by flipping:

ViewsYou drawFlipped copies
11—
41, 2, 34 (from 2)
81 – 56 (from 4), 7 (from 3), 8 (from 2)
161 – 910 – 16 (from 8 down to 2)

View 1 (front) and, with an even count, the back view are their own mirrors. Turn Mirror symmetry on in the demo above and watch the dashed views: those are the ones you never draw.

Mirroring flips the whole finished frame, so anything lopsided flips too. A sword in the right hand becomes a sword in the left hand in every flipped view. The little knight in the demo shows it. If that matters, turn mirroring off and draw every view.

One square canvas, feet on the bottom row

Every frame of every view is drawn on one square canvas. You pick its size when you make the sprite: 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, 96, 128 or 256 pixels, or a custom size from 8 to 256. The size is fixed after that. The character's feet go on the bottom row, and an orange ground line under the canvas marks it. The game hangs the sprite from its feet, so a character drawn floating will float in the game too.

Make a sprite from the studio

Open 🎞 Sprites from the rail or from Home. The page shows every sheet in the module as a tile:

  • A tile marked ◇ is a sheet with no ThreeSprite project behind it: it was imported as a finished atlas, so there is nothing to edit.
  • Click a tile to see its card: how many animations it has, which props draw it, and whether it was baked from a project.
  • A sheet with a project has ✎ edit in ThreeSprite. When more than one editor can open it, open with… lets you choose.

  1. Press + new sprite. The studio asks for the new sheet's name (A-Z, 0-9, _). Type BLOB and confirm. Lower-case letters are turned into capitals, and anything else is dropped.

  2. ThreeSprite opens on an empty project and shows the New sprite dialog. Leave Name and Sprite id as they are.

  3. Under Canvas size, pick 32. Under Views, pick 8. Leave Mirror symmetry ticked: the line reads draw only 5 of 8 views; the rest are flipped automatically.

  4. Press Create. The project starts with one bone called body, one part called body covering the whole canvas, and one animation called idle (4 frames at 8 fps, looping), so you can draw straight away.

  5. With the Pencil, draw a blob standing on the bottom row in view 1. Then press . to go to view 2 and draw it again, a little turned.

  6. Press ← studio in the bar above ThreeSprite. The studio saves the project into the module and bakes it. BLOB now has a tile on the Sprites page with no ◇.

The view count and mirroring can be changed later in Project settings. Fewer views drops the images drawn for views that no longer exist, and the dialog warns you before it does.

What the studio keeps, and what the game reads

  • The source is the ThreeSprite project, kept at sources/sprites/NAME.tsprite.
  • The bake makes what the game draws: assets/sprites/NAME.json (the atlas manifest) and NAME.png (the packed sheet). It runs when you press ← studio, and again before Play and Export for any source that changed.
  • Props and monsters pick a sheet by name. Re-bake a sheet under the same name and everything drawn with it changes.

You can also bring in work made elsewhere with + import a sprite (.tsprite, or an atlas). A .tsprite is kept as the source and baked. An atlas (its .json, then its .png) is taken as a finished sheet and gets the ◇ mark.

Inside the studio, ThreeSprite is slightly different

  • Its project menu (☰) has no New project…, Open… or Save as…: the studio decides which file you are editing.
  • ⌘S saves only a draft, which survives a reload. ThreeSprite also keeps a draft by itself every few seconds while you edit.
  • ← studio is what saves into the module and bakes. The ✕ beside it discards your changes and goes back.

Leaving with ✕ throws the session's changes away. If you are not sure, use ← studio: an unwanted change can be undone on the studio's page afterwards.

Running ThreeSprite on its own

ThreeSprite is also a standalone app. From its own repository:

pnpm install
pnpm dev          # browser build on http://localhost:1420
pnpm dev:web      # the browser-only build, on port 1421
pnpm tauri dev    # the desktop app

There is also a VS Code extension that opens .tsprite files in an editor tab. On its own, ThreeSprite keeps projects in its library and saves .tsprite files; its Export tab writes the sheets. Inside the studio you never need Export, because the studio bakes.

Finding your way around

The top bar

From left to right:

  • ☰ — the project menu: Save, Import PNG as part…, and (outside the studio) new, open and recent projects.
  • The project's name, with a • while there are unsaved changes, then its canvas size, view count and mirrored, for example 32px · 8 views · mirrored. Click it to open Project settings.
  • Undo and Redo (⌘Z, ⇧⌘Z or ⌘Y).
  • The mode tabs, in the middle.
  • ? — every shortcut and gesture — and the settings gear, which opens Project settings too.

A sprite has 8 views. The camera stands exactly at the character's left side. Which view does it see?

  • View 7
  • View 3
  • View 5

Views go anticlockwise seen from above, starting with view 1 in front. A quarter turn round is (3−1)/8 = ¼, and that puts the camera at the character's left, with the character facing screen-left.

You make a 16-view sprite with mirror symmetry on. How many views do you draw?

  • 8
  • 16
  • 9

Mirroring draws ⌊16/2⌋+1 = 9 views: the front, the back and the seven on one side. Views 10 to 16 are flipped copies of views 8 down to 2.
