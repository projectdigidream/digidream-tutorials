Animating

You animate the rig, not the pictures. Keys on bones turn, move and scale them; keys on parts show, hide and swap them. Because the bones are 3D, one set of keys animates every view at once.

Animations

A sprite holds any number of animations, each with its own frame count, speed and loop. A new sprite has one called idle: 4 frames at 8 fps, looping. The left end of the timeline's transport bar manages them:

  • A drop-down of the sprite's animations.
  • + Add animation asks for a name (it suggests anim 2, anim 3 …).
  • Pencil Rename animation and trash Delete animation. Deleting removes its keys, and is undoable.

Then come the controls for the one you chose:

Play / pausePlays the animation on the canvas. Space does the same in the Animate and 3D tabs.

[ and ] step to the previous and next frame, wrapping round.

The timeline

The timeline has one row per bone (as a tree, the same as the Bones panel) and then one row per part. Each column is a frame, and the header numbers them from 1.

  • Click or drag along the header to scrub. It pauses playback.
  • Click a cell to go to that frame.
  • A ◆ in a cell is a key.

Setting keys

There are two ways to set a key on the current frame:

  1. On the canvas: drag a bone, as in the Rig tab. Dragging the tip sets a rotation key, and dragging the root sets a move key. The drag works in the plane of the view you are looking at.
  2. In the Inspector, now titled Keys at frame: type values for the selected bone. It says Frame f of n at the top.

For a bone the Inspector shows three groups, each with a ◆ badge that is lit when there is a key at this frame. Click the badge to set or remove that key.

  • Rot yaw, Rot pitch, Rot roll (degrees)
  • Move right, Move up, Move forward (pixels)
  • Scale right, Scale up, Scale forward

Under them:

  • Ease chooses how this frame's keys blend to the next ones. It is greyed out when the bone has no key at this frame.
  • Key all keys rotation, move and scale at this frame, with their current values.
  • Delete keys removes all three at this frame.

Ease: linear, easeInOut, step

The key at the start of a stretch decides how it gets to the next key:

EaseBetween this key and the nextGood for
linearmoves at an even speedwalk cycles, steady swings
easeInOutstarts slowly, speeds up, slows into the next keybreathing, a weight being lifted
stepholds this key's value, then jumpssnappy, Doom-like poses; anything that must not blend

Sprites often have only 4 to 8 frames, and blending between so few can look mushy. Try step on every key: each frame is then exactly the pose you set.

Parts in the timeline

Select a part (in its timeline row or in Parts) and the Inspector shows two more keys for it. Both are always step, since a part can't be half visible or half one variant.

  • Visible: a tick box. Untick it to hide the part from this frame on. Use it for a muzzle flash, or for a part that only shows in one pose.
  • Variant: which of the part's variants is shown. Use it to open a hand, or to open a mouth to shout.

The onion skin

With the onion skin on, the previous frame is drawn faded under the current one. On frame 1 the "previous" frame is the last one, so the loop point can be checked as well. Try it below: a four-frame bounce, with frame 1 squashed on the ground, then a stretch going up, round at the top, and a stretch coming down.

Copying keys

  1. Click a key, and Shift-click or ⌘-click more. On a touch screen, turn on the multi-select button first and then tap each key.
  2. Press Copy, or ⌘C.
  3. Go to another frame and press Paste, or ⌘V. The keys land at the current frame, with the same spacing between them and the same ease.

Keys don't wrap round the loop. Before the first key a bone holds the first key's value, and after the last key it holds the last one. When a looping animation reaches its last frame it jumps back to frame 1. So key the last frame as the step before frame 1's pose, not as frame 1's pose itself, or the loop shows the same pose twice and stutters.

Exercise: a bob

  1. Open Animate. Choose idle in the drop-down, set frames to 4 and fps to 6, and make sure loop is ticked.

  2. On frame 1, select the body bone in the timeline and press Key all.

  3. Press ] twice to reach frame 3. Type -2 in Move up. A key appears on frame 3.

  4. Press ] to reach frame 4 and type -1 in Move up. That is the step on the way back to frame 1.

  5. Press Space to play. The body bobs in every view: press . while it plays to see.

  6. Click the key on frame 1 and set its Ease to step. Play again: frame 2 now holds frame 1's pose.

  7. Click the key on frame 3, Shift-click the one on frame 4, and press ⌘C. Set frames to 6, go to frame 5 and press ⌘V. Then press ⌘Z twice to put things back.

  8. Turn on the onion skin, pause, and step through with [ and ].

Every bone has a key on every frame, all linear. That is how a script generated them. When you animate by hand you set far fewer keys and let the in-betweens do the work. The result plays the same.

How the slime squashes

SLIME has one bone, so all its life is in the Scale and Move keys of body:

  • idle (8 frames, 8 fps) breathes between Scale right 1.15 / Scale up 0.77 on frame 1 and 1.06 / 0.89 on frame 5. Wide and low, then a little taller.
  • walk (8 frames, 10 fps) is a hop: squashed flat on frame 1 (1.3 wide, 0.65 tall), stretched tall on frame 3 (0.99 / 0.98) as it leaves the ground, highest on frame 5 (Move up 7.5), and squashed hardest on frame 8 (1.35 / 0.61) as it lands.
  • death (8 frames) flattens it into a puddle, down to 0.13 tall and 1.54 wide, and holds there.

The rule of squash and stretch: when it gets wider, make it shorter, so it seems to keep its volume.

How the coin spins

COIN has no bones that move. Its one animation, spin, is 6 frames at 6 fps, looping, with one Variant key on each frame of the picture part: face, w75, w40, edge, b40, b75. Variant keys are always step, so each frame shows exactly one drawing.

The other pickups mix the two ways. GEM and KEY have one picture and bob: a Move up key on root rises to 9 px on frame 5 of 8 and comes back. STAR also swaps between variants s100, s105 and s110, slightly bigger drawings, so it pulses as it bobs. TORCH flickers between two variants at 6 fps. The LEVER has off and on, one frame each, and pull, six frames turning the handle from one side to the other.

  1. Open Gem Hollow's SLIME in ThreeSprite and open Animate. Choose walk in the drop-down.

  2. Click body in the timeline and step through with ]. Watch Scale right, Scale up and Move up change in Keys at frame.

  3. Go to frame 8 and type 0.4 in Scale up. Play with Space: the landing splats harder.

  4. Press ✕ to throw the change away.

In Cinderfall

Cinderfall, the first-person shooter example, comes with the studio: on Home, under ◆ examples, press 📂 Cinderfall. The Cinderfall tour walks round it.

A shooter needs animations a platformer does not: an aim before the shot, and for the boss, moves of its own. Every one is seen from 8 views, so each pose has to read from the back as well as the front.

The heavy ones move slowly: SMELTER and BRUTE walk at 7 fps where GUNNER walks at 10 and HOUND at 14. The same 8 frames at a lower speed make a heavier step.

A monster with more than five animations is wired by number: each of SMELTER's eight is a state line in assets/props.txt (idle is state 0, walk 1, aim 2, pain 3, death 4, fire 5, slam 6, roar 7), and its brain asks for anim = 7 when it roars. See Attacks.

The revolver in your hand

VM_REVOLVER has one view and five animations: ready (8@6, looping), draw (5@14), fire (6@18), cock (5@14) and reload (14@12).

  • ready sways: rig's Move right goes 0, 0.42, 0.6, 0.42, 0, −0.42, −0.6, −0.42 while Move up rises and falls by half a pixel. The gun drifts in a slow figure of eight.
  • draw brings it up from 60 px below, turning from Rot pitch 30 to 0.
  • fire drops the hammer from −40 on frame 1 to 0 on frame 2, kicks rig up to Rot pitch −16 (and 3 px up, 4 towards you) on frame 2, and eases back to rest by frame 6.
  • cock pulls the hammer back to −45 by frame 3, then lets it settle at −40, ready to fire; reload rolls the gun over (Rot roll 70) to show the cylinder, which turns and swings out.

Every animation also keys rig's Scale at 3.4 on every frame: the gun is modelled small and blown up to fill the 128 px canvas. The keys are added to the rest pose, so Rot yaw 0 in Keys at frame means the rig's own −20.

The muzzle flash is the flash bone, keyed by Scale: 1 on the first frame of fire, 0.6 on the second, and 0.01, too small to see, on every other frame of every animation. A Visible key on the flash part would do the same job, and is the simpler way when you make your own.

  1. Open Cinderfall's VM_REVOLVER in ThreeSprite and open Animate. Choose fire and step through with ]: the flash on frames 1 and 2, the kick on frame 2.

  2. Click rig in the timeline, go to frame 2 and set Rot pitch to −30. Play with Space: a much heavier gun.

  3. Open SMELTER and choose roar. Step round it with . while it plays: check the pose reads from the back too. Leave both with ✕.

What the studio does with animations

Each animation becomes one animation in the baked sheet, with its frame count, fps and loop. Whether it loops can also be set on the sheet's card in the studio, and that choice survives every bake (see Which animations loop). A monster plays one animation per state: in the monster editor, Looks → Each state plays picks it by name. When a sheet is chosen, the studio guesses the first five body states from the names:

StateAn animation whose name contains
idleidle or stand
walkwalk, run or move
attackattack, fire or melee
painpain or hurt (else the idle one)
deathdeath or die

If nothing matches, the first animation is used. You can change any of them on Looks afterwards.

Name your animations idle, walk, attack, pain and death, and a new monster is wired up the moment you give it the sheet. Gem Hollow's SLIME, BEE and SNAIL use idle, walk, attack, hurt and death: hurt matches the pain state.

A bone has a key on frame 1 set to step, and another on frame 5. What does it do on frames 2 to 4?

  • Moves evenly towards frame 5's pose
  • Eases out of frame 1 and into frame 5
  • Holds frame 1's pose, then jumps on frame 5

The key at the start of a stretch decides how it gets to the next key, and step holds.

You want a muzzle flash only on frame 2 of the attack. What do you key?

  • The flash part's Visible: on at frame 2, off at frames 1 and 3
  • The flash bone's scale to 0 on the other frames
  • A second animation with the flash in it

Visibility keys show and hide a part, and are always step. Keying it off at frame 1 matters, because before the first key a part shows its first key's value.

Gem Hollow's COIN turns with no bones moving. What is keyed?

  • The picture part's Variant: a different drawing on each of 6 frames
  • The root bone's Rot yaw, a full turn
  • Scale right, from 1 down to 0 and back

A one-view sprite can't be turned by its bones: there is no other view to show. So each angle of the coin is drawn as a variant, and spin keys one variant per frame.

You copy keys on frames 1 and 3, then paste with the playhead on frame 5. Where do they land?

  • On frames 1 and 3 again
  • On frames 5 and 7
  • Both on frame 5

Paste puts the first copied frame at the playhead and keeps the spacing between them.
