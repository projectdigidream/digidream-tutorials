Bone animation: walk, swing, fall

With a rig, you don't redraw a character for every frame. You turn its bones on a few key frames, and ThreeSprite works out the frames in between, in every view. This lesson builds the three animations a monster needs most: a walk that loops, an attack swing, and a death.

You'll learnhow a bone's turn travels down to its children; which view to pose in; how ThreeSprite fills the frames between keys; how to make a loop with no hitch; and how to time an attack and a fall Time40 minutes Needsa rigged sprite with a pelvis, torso, head, arms, thighs and shins (see Rig, bones and parts), and the timeline basics from Animating

Try it first

The demo below is a stand-in for ThreeSprite's Animate tab: a side view of a small rig, with a key grid underneath. It works out the in-between frames with the same rules as ThreeSprite. Drag a bone's tip to turn it, and the turn is keyed on the current frame. Load the walk example and press Play, then try the others.

The demo is a flat side view, so each bone has one angle. In ThreeSprite each bone has three (Rot yaw, Rot pitch, Rot roll), and the rig is drawn from every view. The rules for keys and in-betweens are the same.

How a bone moves

Every bone has a rest pose, the one you set in the Rig tab. An animation never changes the rest pose. It stores offsets on top of it, per frame:

TrackInspector fieldsWhat it does
rotationRot yaw · Rot pitch · Rot rollTurns the bone about its root, added to its rest angles. This is almost all of animation.
moveMove right · Move up · Move forwardShifts the bone's root. Use it on the root bone, to bob the body or drop it to the floor.
scaleScale right · Scale up · Scale forwardStretches what hangs on the bone, for squash and stretch or a puff of breath.

The three angles are about the character's own axes:

  • yaw turns about the up axis, like shaking your head;
  • pitch tilts about the right axis, like nodding or swinging a leg forward;
  • roll rotates in the front view's plane, like raising an arm sideways.

Children follow their parents

A bone's turn carries everything below it in the tree. Turn the torso and the head and arms go with it. Turn the thigh and the shin and foot go with it. Each child's own angle is measured from its parent. This is called forward kinematics, and it decides the order you pose in:

  1. Root first. Where is the body: higher, lower, leaning?
  2. Then down each chain: torso, then head; thigh, then shin; upper arm, then forearm.
  3. Small things last: a hand, a weapon, a tail tip.

If you pose a shin and then turn its thigh, the shin keeps its angle to the thigh and goes along. That is usually what you want. Pose the other way round and you'll find yourself fixing the shin again after every thigh change.

Pose in the right view

The Animate tab's hint says it: drag bone handles on the canvas to key them (rotating in the plane of the current view), or type values here. A drag can only turn a bone across your screen. So the view you drag in decides which of the three angles changes:

MotionAnglePose it in
legs stepping, arms swinging, a nod, an overhead choppitcha side view: view 3, Left side, in an 8-view sprite
arms raised sideways, leaning left or right, a sideways sweeprollthe front view: view 1
turning the head or the hips to look roundyawno view shows it as a turn on screen, so type it in Rot yaw

The signs, so typed values do what you expect:

  • positive pitch swings a hanging bone's tip forward (a leg, an arm) and tips an upright bone back (the torso, the head);
  • positive roll turns a bone counter-clockwise on screen in the front view.

Press , and . to change view at any time. The pose is the same 3D pose in every view, so a stride you set in the side view is already right in the other seven.

Shift-dragging a tip changes the bone's length, and this is not keyed. The new length belongs to the rig, so it changes every animation and the rest pose too. In Animate, drag without Shift. To stretch something over time, key Scale.

What happens between keys

You set a few keys, and ThreeSprite computes every other frame. Four rules decide the result:

  1. The key at the start of a stretch decides its ease. With linear the bone moves evenly. With easeInOut it starts slowly, speeds up, and slows into the next key. With step it holds its pose, then jumps on the next key.
  2. Before the first key, a bone holds the first key's pose.
  3. After the last key, it holds the last key's pose.
  4. A bone with no keys stays at rest.

A diamond in the timeline stands for all of that bone's keys on that frame (rotation, move and scale). The Ease you pick for it applies to all three. Key all keys the bone's three tracks as they are now, which is a quick way to pin a pose before you change it later.

The loop seam

Rule 3 has a catch. When an animation loops, playback jumps from the last frame back to frame 1, but the in-betweens do not wrap round. Nothing blends from the last key back to frame 1, so how the loop joins is up to you:

  • If the last key comes before the last frame, the remaining frames hold that pose, and the cycle stalls before it restarts.
  • If the last frame is a copy of frame 1, the same pose shows twice in a row, and the cycle hitches. The demo warns about this one.
  • The fix: make the last frame the step before frame 1, a pose halfway between the frame before it and frame 1. The walk below does this on frame 8.

Exercise: a walk cycle

A walk is two steps, and the second is the first with the legs swapped. Eight frames at 10 fps make a brisk stride:

FramePoseNear legFar legBody
1contact: near heel landsforward, straightbacklow
3passing: far leg swings pastunder the body, nearly straightknee bent, foot liftedhigh
5contact, the other sidebackforward, straightlow
7passing, the other sideknee bent, foot liftedunder the bodyhigh
8the step back into frame 1halfway between 7 and 1

  1. In Animate, add an animation named walk. Set frames to 8 and fps to 10, and tick loop. The name matters: the studio's monster card picks an animation whose name contains walk, run or move for walking.

  2. Press . until the canvas shows the side view (view 3 in an 8-view sprite). You'll pose all the pitch here.

  3. Frame 1, contact. Drag the near thigh's tip forward, to about Rot pitch 25, and the far thigh back, to about −25. Leave the near shin straight and bend the far shin a touch, about −5. Swing the near arm back and the far arm forward: arms go against the legs.

  4. Frame 3, passing. Press ] twice. Bring the near thigh under the body (about 0). Swing the far thigh forward a little (about 5) and bend its shin hard (about −45), so the foot clears the floor. Arms at about 0. Select the root bone and type Move up 1: the body rises as it passes over the planted leg.

  5. Frame 5, contact again. Copy frame 1's diamonds (click them in the timeline, Shift-click to add more, press ⌘C), go to frame 5 and press ⌘V. Then swap the sides by hand: the near leg takes the far leg's numbers and the far leg the near's. Do the same for the arms.

  6. Frame 7, passing again. Copy frame 3 to frame 7, then swap the legs and arms the same way. Keep Move up 1 on the root.

  7. Frame 8, the seam. Key every moving bone halfway between its frame 7 and frame 1 values. For example, the near thigh goes from 5 at frame 7 to 25 at frame 1, so 15 at frame 8. The root's Move up is 0.5.

  8. Press Space. Watch for a stall or a hitch where the loop joins. Then turn on the onion skin, pause, and step with [ and ]. Each frame should move the feet about the same distance.

  9. Press . while it plays and walk round the views. The front and back views show the arms and legs swinging towards and away from you. If the sprite has Depth scaling on, the near foot grows a little as it comes forward.

The feet slide. A sprite walks on the spot, and the game moves it across the floor. If its feet seem to skate, the stride and the monster's speed don't match. Shorten or lengthen the stride (the thigh angles), or change the fps, rather than fixing it in the game.

Exercise: an overhead swing

An attack reads well with three beats. First a slow wind-up the player can see coming, then a strike so fast it nearly isn't drawn, then a recovery. You make those beats with the ease of each key, not with more frames. Load overhead swing in the demo above to see the finished version.

  1. Add an animation named attack with 7 frames at 10 fps, and untick loop. An attack plays once.

  2. Frame 1: select the arm and the torso and press Key all on each, to pin the start pose. Set both keys' Ease to easeInOut, so the wind-up starts slowly.

  3. Frame 3, wind-up: swing the arm up and back over the head, and lean the torso back. In the side view that is about −150 and 10 of pitch. Set these keys to step, then key the same pose again on frame 4. The pose now holds on frames 3 and 4. This is anticipation: the moment the player reads "it's coming".

  4. Frame 5, strike: whip the arm down and forward (about 70) and throw the torso forward (about −18). Because frame 4's key is linear and only one frame away, the strike is a single frame of motion. Set frame 5 to step and key frame 6 with the same pose: the follow-through holds.

  5. Frame 6 → 7, recover: set frame 6 to easeInOut, and on frame 7 bring the arm and torso most of the way back to rest.

  6. Play it. Then try frame 4 at easeInOut in place of linear, and see how a soft strike loses its punch.

For a flash on the strike frame, key a part's Visible on frame 5 only. See Parts in the timeline.

Exercise: a death that stays down

A death plays once and must end on the floor. The rules work for you here: after the last key the pose holds, so the body stays down for as long as the game shows it. Load death in the demo to see it.

  1. Add an animation named death, 8 frames at 8 fps, loop unticked.

  2. Frame 1: Key all on the root, the torso, the arm and the legs, with easeInOut, so the hit starts with a jolt and then gives.

  3. Frame 3: the knees buckle. Bend the thighs forward and the shins back, and lean the torso back. Key the root's Move up down a few pixels.

  4. Frame 6: lying down. The torso is nearly flat (about 85 of pitch, back), the legs are straight out, and the root is lowered until the body touches the floor line.

  5. Frames 7 and 8: a small bounce. Key the root up a pixel on frame 7 and back down on frame 8. Weight is in the settle.

  6. Play it. When it reaches frame 8 it stops there, because a non-looping animation holds its last frame.

The studio guesses the death state from a name containing death or die, and pain from pain or hurt. A good pain animation is the first two frames of the death with a quick recovery, 3 or 4 frames long.

Before you go back to the studio

  • Look at every view. In the 3D tab, turn up Spread to see every view at once while the animation plays. A pose that reads from the side can hide a limb from the front.
  • Check the layer order. When an arm swings in front of the body in one view and behind it in another, set the part's order in that view's own scope. See Layer order, per view.
  • Count the frames. Every frame of every animation becomes a frame in the baked atlas, in every authored view. Eight frames are often plenty, and a 4-frame idle is fine.
  • Press ← studio. The sprite is baked, and the monster's body card can pick idle, walk, attack, pain and death from your names.

You pose a walk in the front view by dragging the thighs, but the legs only splay sideways. Why?

  • The thighs have no keys yet
  • The animation needs more frames
  • A drag turns a bone in the plane of the current view; a stride is pitch, so pose it in a side view

In the front view a drag changes roll, a sideways turn. Stepping forward and back is pitch, which only shows as a turn on screen in a side view.

Your 8-frame walk has keys on frames 1, 3, 5 and 7, and loop is on. What happens on frame 8?

  • It blends from frame 7 back towards frame 1
  • It holds frame 7's pose, so the cycle stalls for a frame
  • It shows frame 1's pose

After the last key a bone holds that key's pose, and looping doesn't wrap the in-betweens. Key frame 8 yourself, halfway between 7 and 1.

The wind-up pose is on frames 3 and 4, and the strike is on frame 5. Which ease on frame 4's key gives the hardest hit?

  • linear: one even frame of motion, straight into the strike
  • easeInOut: it slows into the strike
  • step: frame 4 holds, then frame 5 jumps

Step would work too, but it skips the motion entirely. Linear over one frame gives a fast, readable strike, and easeInOut softens the arrival, which is what you don't want in a hit.

In the Animate tab you Shift-drag the arm's tip to make a punch reach further. What changed?

  • A scale key on this frame
  • A move key on this frame
  • The arm bone's length, in every animation and the rest pose

Length belongs to the rig, not to an animation. Undo it, and key Scale up on the arm for a stretch over time.
